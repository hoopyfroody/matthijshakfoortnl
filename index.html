Agents write any code you ask for. Knowing what to ask for is the hard part.

Finding out what to build, defining what “good” means, and helping the agent and the people decide which code a person still has to read. These are the responsibilities of a Product Engineer. I do that work in your team, I teach your team to do it, and I help you train the juniors who will do it next.

What is a Product Engineer?

Writing code got cheap. The work an agent cannot do (well) did not, and a Product Engineer is responsible for it:

  • Finding out what users and customers actually need
  • Converting that into a spec an agent can build from
  • Defining “good” and “delightful”, and checking it
  • Deciding which risks to accept and which tradeoffs to make
  • Determining which code a person must still read before it goes live

In short: the product and engineering work that needs a human touch. I believe this role will become essential for teams that want to work well with agents.

01 Hands-on Product Engineering

I build software that supports how your team works

Before agents, a lot of custom software ideas cost too much or took too long to build. The point where these tools pay themselves back has shifted, though. The expensive part is still the work around the code: finding out what the process really needs, writing the spec, and building the checks.

When integrating an LLM: plain code should do everything plain code can do. The model only gets the part it is good at: judgement and text comprehension.

Tooling that makes your team more efficient

The right tooling takes the friction out of your team’s work process, so your team spends its time on product engineering. Some of it your team should still buy, but the breakpoint has shifted. More software can now be specific to your team or company, and that part I build or set up.

Your team buys

  • The coding agent itself
  • A hosted AI review service
  • Hosting and sandboxes for background agents
  • LLM observability
  • Preview deployments

I build or set up

  • Instruction files and project templates
  • Skills for recurring work: releases, migrations, incident notes, converting specs to tickets, and much more
  • Hooks, to prevent the agent from going off-track: formatting, blocked commands, protected paths
  • Custom linters and structural tests
  • The review policy (which code a person reads) and a reviewer that applies it
  • Eval sets and CI gates
  • Internal MCP servers and CLIs for your own systems
  • A framework for continuous improvement

That tooling is what supports your team and lets them continue working in this new way after I leave. The goal is to make myself redundant.

Build it in-house, or not?

Small tools got cheap to build. Owning them did not. Here are three questions to help you decide what to do.

Do people depend on it?

Can it change or expose important data?

Would work stop or slow significantly if it disappeared?

Answer the three questions to see what it needs.

After handover

Your own people own the software. They get the tests, a written spec, the conventions and, where an LLM is involved, an eval set, so the next person or agent can work in it safely. Your team runs it. I am happy to stay on call for the moments when they want a second opinion.

02 Forming Product Engineers

Your own people grow into the role

Training, coaching and leading by example. Leading by example happens while doing the job, so this often starts as hands-on work. Three things often go wrong as teams start this new way of working, and I coach your team on how to avoid them:

  • Getting the agent to understand. The agent builds something good, but not what you meant, often because nobody could clearly communicate what the user actually wants to the agent.
  • AFK agents or human-in-the-loop? Hours or even days lost to an agent misinterpreting the user's intent, or to babysitting agents that did not need it. Choosing the right level of automation is hard.
  • Keeping apps maintainable for humans and agents. The agent builds something that works perfectly, but the human cannot understand it. The human makes manual changes that are then considered gospel by the agent, hobbling future work.

One loop for every change

  1. Talk to the user and write the spec the agent builds from.
  2. Judge the blast radius: let the agent run alone or stay with it, and choose the code a person reads.
  3. Write down each convention the work turned up, for people and agents alike.

I run this loop with your team until it runs without me.

Typical engagement
One or two days a week with one team for three months. First I do the work while they watch, then they do it while I coach.
Case sketch
Previous employer, as software architect. I researched how the team could use coding agents well. I gave presentations, wrote standard operating procedures and tooling: an editor workflow, ways of working with tests, and shared skills. The editor workflow is still in daily use. The skills have been replaced by updated ones, which is exactly how the loop above is supposed to work.

03 Educating Juniors

Where do your seniors come from in five years?

Juniors used to learn by writing code from strict tickets. Agents do that work now, so training juniors on tickets trains them for the job agents already do.

Already have juniors? Now that agents do the tickets, they need different first tasks, and a team that is set up to give them.

I start them on the work agents cannot do: talking to users and customers, writing specs, choosing test seams, judging blast radius. They read and run code from day one, through the spec they wrote and the tests that check it. Hand-coding skills for the critical parts come later.

I also help set up the team around them: what juniors work on first, who they talk to, and how their specs get reviewed.

An interview that tests that work

  1. A 20-minute conversation with a “user”, played by someone on your team.
  2. A one-page spec and test cases.
  3. A review of code an agent built from that spec.

What I look for

  • They ask “why” and “for whom” before “how”.
  • They write clearly.
  • They notice when something feels wrong in a demo.
  • They are comfortable talking to people who are not developers.
  • They read code critically.
Typical engagement
Your next junior hiring round. I design the assignment and sit on the panel, then I coach the new hire one day a week through their first three months.
Evidence
I have mentored somewhere between ten and twenty juniors. Recently I took a few of them along this route instead of the old one. The early results are promising.
